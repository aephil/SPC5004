Fine-tuning language models, choice by choice

In lectures 1–5 we took the transformer apart. In lectures 6–10 we took pretrained transformers and adapted them to our own tasks. This page follows two projects through the choices that involves: a classifier that sorts tweets into topics (lab 6), and a chatbot made from a base language model (lab 9).

Each choice trades off accuracy, memory, storage and time. The strip below the diagram records the choices made so far for each project.

Start from a pretrained model

  • Pretraining on a huge, general corpus is expensive, and is done once by someone with a lot of compute. The weights then encode a lot about language.
  • Fine-tuning adapts that model to our own task, with far less data and compute. This is transfer learning.
  • Every fine-tuning run has the same shape: data goes in at the bottom, through the model and a head, to a loss at the top. Training sends the gradient of the loss back down (dashed orange arrow).
  • We follow two projects: a classifier and a chatbot.

Choose an architecture and a base model

  • To label or understand text, use an encoder, which reads the whole input at once. To generate text, use a decoder. Encoder-decoders map one sequence to another, as in translation.
  • Bigger models are usually better, but cost more memory and time to train and to run. Small models are fast and cheap, and can run privately on your own hardware.
  • Classifier: DistilBERT, with 66M parameters. Chatbot: SmolLM2-1.7B.
  • We start from the base SmolLM2, not the -Instruct version, to see what fine-tuning adds.
  • The memory gauge shows the weights alone: 0.27 GB in 32 bits for DistilBERT, 3.42 GB in 16 bits for SmolLM2-1.7B.

Add components

  • An encoder outputs a vector per token, not a label. The classifier gets a new head: two linear layers that read the [CLS] vector, then a softmax over the 6 topics (num_labels=6).
  • The head starts from random weights (dashed outline), so it has everything to learn.
  • Other tasks need other heads: a label for each token (to find names to redact, say), or a single score (step 12).
  • The chatbot needs nothing new: its language-model head already predicts the next token.

Choose what to train

  • Full fine-tuning updates every weight, including any new head (orange outline). With the Adam optimiser in 32 bits, each trained parameter costs 16 bytes: the weight, its gradient and two running averages. The activations saved for the backward pass come on top.
  • For the classifier that is about 1.1 GB, so we train it in full. For the chatbot it would be about 27 GB (hatched bar): far too much for an 8 GB GPU.
  • LoRA freezes the pretrained weights $W$ (grey) and learns a small low-rank update, $W' = W + BA$. At rank $r = 8$, only 1.6M parameters train: under 0.1% of the model.
  • LoRA also saves storage: a 6.3 MB adapter instead of a full copy. The activations are still needed, so the memory saving is less than 0.1% suggests.

Choose what to quantise

  • Quantisation stores weights in fewer bits: 8 or 4 instead of 16 or 32. A scale factor maps each block of weights onto a handful of integer levels.
  • It saves memory, at some cost in accuracy (step 10 measures it).
  • QLoRA quantises the frozen base model to 4 bits, and trains LoRA adapters on top at higher precision. Gradient checkpointing saves more memory by recomputing some activations instead of storing them.
  • Training the chatbot this way peaked at 2.24 GB. The classifier is small enough already.

Choose the training data

  • What: tweets labelled with their topic; conversations between a user and an assistant (no_robots).
  • How much: 4,374 tweets; 9,500 conversations. Quality and variety matter as much as quantity.
  • Splits: keep separate training, validation and test sets. Validation guides choices during training; the test set is used once, at the end. Mixing them makes results look better than they are.
  • Batches: each update averages the gradient over a batch. Bigger batches give steadier gradients but need more memory. Gradient accumulation adds up several small batches before each update: the chatbot uses 4 × 2, an effective batch of 8.

Supervised fine-tuning: a special case

  • Training on conversations is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). A conversation has to become one token sequence. A chat template marks each turn with special tokens, such as <|im_start|>user … <|im_end|>.
  • The template must match the one used when chatting. Our base model has none, so we borrow the -Instruct model's.
  • Loss masking: the prompt is only input (label −100). The loss counts the reply, including its final <|im_end|>, which is how the model learns to stop.
  • With several assistant turns, we can train on all of them, or only on the last.
  • SFT mostly teaches the form of a reply; the knowledge comes from pretraining.

Choose the hyperparameters

  • Learning rate $\eta$: too small and training creeps; too large and it overshoots or diverges.
  • Schedule: warm up from zero over the first few steps, then decay, for example along a cosine.
  • Weight decay: a small pull of every weight towards zero, against overfitting.
  • Length: a number of epochs (passes over the data), or a fixed number of steps.
  • Classifier: $\eta = 5 \times 10^{-5}$, linear decay, 2 epochs. Chatbot: $\eta = 2 \times 10^{-4}$, 30 warmup steps then cosine, weight decay 0.1, 300 steps.
  • There is no formula for these values. We try some and read the loss curves.

Read the loss curves

  • Plot the training loss and the validation loss against the step number.
  • Healthy: both fall, then level off together.
  • Underfitting: both stay high. Train for longer, raise the learning rate, or use a bigger model.
  • Overfitting: the training loss keeps falling while the validation loss rises, because the model is memorising its training data. Stop earlier, get more data, or regularise.
  • In lab 6, the second epoch cut the training loss from 0.64 to 0.29, but the validation loss rose from 0.42 to 0.48: the start of overfitting.

Evaluate: intrinsic metrics

  • An intrinsic metric measures the model itself, with no task or labels. For a language model, that is the loss on held-out text.
  • Perplexity = exp(mean loss). SmolLM2-135M's mean loss on "The cat sat on the mat" is 4.33, so its perplexity is about 76: as uncertain as choosing among 76 equally likely tokens. Lower is better.
  • Compare like with like: the same text, tokeniser and context length.
  • Perplexity shows what our choices cost. Quantising SmolLM2-1.7B to 4 bits raises its perplexity on WikiText-2 from 10.7 to 12.4, still better than the 16-bit 360M model (15.3). Fine-tuning SmolLM2-135M on Shakespeare raised its WikiText-2 perplexity from 20.5 to 47.3.
  • Real values from lab 10.

Evaluate: extrinsic metrics

  • An extrinsic metric measures performance on the task itself, and needs labelled examples.
  • Classifier: a confusion matrix counts predictions against true labels. Precision: of the tweets predicted "science", how many are? Recall: of the science tweets, how many were found? F1 combines the two. Unlike accuracy, it isn't fooled by a rare class. Lab 6 reached F1 = 0.87 on the validation set.
  • Benchmarks often use multiple-choice questions, scored by which answer the model finds most probable.
  • Chatbot: open-ended replies have no single right answer. Compare them with reference texts (BLEU, ROUGE), or ask another model or people to judge. Our adapter answers in the right form, but invents facts.
  • Real replies from lab 9 (SmolLM2-1.7B), truncated.

Feed preferences back into training

  • If the real test is "which reply do people prefer?", collect that as data: a prompt with a preferred reply ✓ and a rejected one ✗.
  • RLHF: train a reward model (a pretrained model with a scalar head, step 3) to score replies as people would. Then reinforcement learning steers the chatbot towards high scores.
  • DPO: train the chatbot on the pairs directly, with no reward model.
  • SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct was made this way: SFT on about a million conversations, then preference tuning.
