How good is a language model?

So far we’ve been training against cross-entropy. Perplexity is a more interpretable way of considering the same number:

\[\text{Perplexity} = \exp(\text{mean cross-entropy loss})\]

  • Loosely: the model’s average “branching factor” — how many roughly-equally-likely next tokens it’s choosing among.
  • Lower is better. A perplexity of 1 would mean the model is never in doubt about the next token.
  • It’s computed on held-out text, exactly like a validation loss.

A worked example

SmolLM2-135M reads “The cat sat on the mat” and, at each step, gives a probability to the token that actually comes next:

next token \(p\) \(-\ln p\) top guess
cat 0.00014 8.84 first (0.02)
sat 0.0031 5.76 is (0.09)
on 0.23 1.46 on
the 0.60 0.52 the
mat 0.0062 5.08 bed (0.07)
mean 4.33
  • Mean cross-entropy loss: 4.33 nats; perplexity \(e^{4.33} \approx 76\).
  • Equivalently, \(\text{PPL} = \left(\prod_i p_i\right)^{-1/N}\): one over the geometric mean of the probabilities.
  • As if choosing among 76 equally likely tokens at every step.
  • A few surprises dominate: with no context, “cat” after “The” is a 1-in-7,000 shot.

How to read the scale

  • 1: the model is certain, and right, every time.
  • \(V\), the vocabulary size (49,152 for SmolLM2): no better than guessing uniformly from the whole vocabulary.
  • Above \(V\): confidently wrong — worse than knowing nothing at all.
  • Because perplexity is an exponential, differences in loss become ratios of perplexity: a drop of 0.1 in loss is about a 10% drop in perplexity, wherever you start.

A meaningful evaluation needs a corpus

A single passage of a hundred or so tokens gives a noisy estimate. Real evaluations pool over a whole held-out corpus:

\[\text{PPL}_\text{corpus} = \exp\left(\frac{\text{total negative log-likelihood}}{\text{total number of tokens predicted}}\right)\]

Don’t average perplexities. Two passages, one of 10 tokens with perplexity 100 and one of 1,000 tokens with perplexity 10:

  • Mean of the perplexities: \((100 + 10)/2 = 55\).
  • Pooled: \(\exp\left(\frac{10 \ln 100 + 1000 \ln 10}{1010}\right) \approx 10.2\) — dominated, correctly, by the passage with almost all the tokens.

Comparing perplexities fairly

A perplexity on its own means little; it’s useful for comparing models. To be fair, compare on:

  • The same text. Perplexity depends as much on the text as on the model: Shakespeare is harder to predict than a weather report.
  • The same tokeniser. Perplexity is per token: a tokeniser that cuts text into more, smaller pieces makes each prediction easier. (Normalising per character or per byte avoids this.)
  • The same context length. Text is evaluated in blocks; tokens near the start of a block have little context and are harder to predict.
  • Uncontaminated text. If the test text was in the pretraining data — Wikipedia, Shakespeare — a low perplexity partly measures memory.

Perplexity and model size

  • Bigger models, trained on more data, predict text better.
  • Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022): loss falls smoothly and predictably with parameters and training tokens, roughly as a power law.
  • But with diminishing returns: each tenfold increase in size buys a smaller absolute improvement than the last.
  • The exception: if the test text was in the training data, a big enough model can memorise it, and perplexity then falls faster than scaling laws predict.
  • In the lab: SmolLM2 at 135M, 360M and 1.7B parameters — same tokeniser, similar training data — on Shakespeare and on WikiText-2.

Perplexity as a measure of damage

Perplexity can also measure what a change to a model costs. Quantising SmolLM2-1.7B, as in the lecture on parameter-efficient fine-tuning:

precision measured memory footprint
16-bit 3.4 GB
8-bit 1.8 GB
4-bit NF4 1.0 GB

(More than the bare 4- or 8-bit weights: some parts, such as the embeddings, stay in 16 bits.) Rounding the weights changes the predictions slightly, so perplexity rises. In the lab you’ll measure by how much, and ask: is a 4-bit 1.7B model better than a 16-bit 360M model (about 0.7 GB)?

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic evaluation

  • Intrinsic: perplexity measures how well the model predicts held-out text, without reference to any particular downstream use. No task-specific data or labels needed.
  • Extrinsic: the accuracy, precision/recall and F1 metrics from the classification lecture measure performance on a specific task — they need labelled task data, but tell you something more directly useful for that task.

A model can have excellent perplexity yet still be middling at some specific downstream task, and vice versa — the two families of metric answer different questions.

Benchmarks without generating anything

Many benchmarks are multiple choice, scored with the same ingredient as perplexity: the probability of a continuation.

Question: Which of these is a renewable source of energy?
Answer: coal / wind / oil / natural gas

  • Compute \(\log p(\text{wind} \mid \text{question})\), and the same for every other choice.
  • The model “answers” with whichever choice is most probable; accuracy is the fraction it gets right.
  • No generated text to parse — but longer answers have lower probability, so scores are often normalised by length.
  • This is how tools like lm-evaluation-harness score benchmarks such as ARC and HellaSwag.

Other evaluation metrics

Beyond perplexity and classification metrics, generation is often scored with:

  • BLEU / ROUGE: compare generated text against one or more reference texts by measuring word/phrase overlap — common for translation and summarisation.
  • Standard benchmark suites (collections of tasks with fixed test sets and leaderboards) let different models be compared on a level footing.
  • LLM-as-a-judge: another, stronger model scores or compares the outputs. Cheap and flexible, but with biases of its own.
  • Human preference: people vote between pairs of anonymous replies, as in Chatbot Arena.
